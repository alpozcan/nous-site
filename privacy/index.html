Nous AI Newsletter: Privacy Policy

Nous AI Newsletter needs no account, and asks who you are only if you choose to create one, for email. This is exactly what the app sends, what the server keeps, and for how long.

Last updated 2026-10-23

The short version. Nous needs no account. Everything in the app works without one and there is no password. From version 2.0 you can choose to create an optional account with Sign in with Apple, only to get email from Nous: the Monthly AI News Brief and announcements, each switched off until you switch it on. Nothing has been sent to anyone yet. Unless you create that account, the app does not ask who you are and the server has no way of finding out. It keeps one anonymous identifier per installed copy of the app so that it can tell a real copy of the app from a script, and, only if you switch notifications on, the settings a notification needs. It contains no advertising and no third-party trackers, nothing records what you read, and nothing about you is sold or shared for advertising. From version 2.0, each time the app opens it also contacts RevenueCat, which keeps subscription statistics for us, whether or not you subscribe, and a record is added when you buy, restore or redeem Nous+. RevenueCat works under an anonymous identifier and never learns your name, email address or Apple ID. If the app crashes, it sends a technical crash report that says nothing about what you were reading and is not tied to you or to your copy of the app. From version 1.2.3 it also measures how quickly its screens load, and you can switch all of this off in Settings, the contact with RevenueCat included. From version 1.2, the spoken summary of the day is written, translated and read aloud on your device; nothing you listen to leaves it. From version 2.0, the companies and models you follow, your reading history and your weekly recap stay on your device too. The one exception is your own choice: if you turn on breaking alerts, the list of what you follow is sent so that the server knows what to alert you about, and it is deleted when you turn them off.

Who is responsible

Nous AI Newsletter is made by Alp Özcan, an individual developer. For anything in this policy, including a request to delete data, write to hi@alp.me. Mail is usually answered within a few days.

No account needed

The app authenticates itself, not you. It uses Apple App Attest: on first launch your device generates a cryptographic key inside its Secure Enclave, and Apple vouches that the key belongs to a genuine, unmodified copy of this app on genuine Apple hardware. Every later request carries a short signature made with that key.

This proves that a request came from the app. It proves nothing about you, and it cannot: the key is created on the device, the private half never leaves it, and no name, email address, phone number or Apple ID is involved at any point.

The one exception is the optional account described next. Nothing in the app depends on it: the free edition, Nous+, notifications and everything else work the same without it, and Nous+ belongs to your Apple ID, not to the account.

Only if you create the optional account (version 2.0 and later)

You can create an account in the app's settings, under Account, from a card the app shows once under the end of today's edition, or from a link at the bottom of the Week in AI. The only way to create it is Sign in with Apple; there is no password and no other kind of login. Nous asks Apple for your email address only, never your name. Apple lets you share your real address or a Hide My Email address that forwards to your inbox; the app shows you which one Nous has.

If you create the account, the server keeps:

  • the email address Apple shared, encrypted, whether it is a Hide My Email address, whether Apple says it is verified, and whether Apple is currently forwarding to it;
  • a keyed fingerprint of the stable account number Apple gives Nous for your Apple ID (the number itself is not stored), so that signing in again finds the same account;
  • a token from Apple, encrypted, that lets Nous tell Apple to disconnect your Apple ID from Nous when you delete the account;
  • which copies of the app are signed in: the account is linked to the anonymous identifier of each copy you sign in on, which is how the app proves it is you without any password or session (at most 20; signing in again on a new device links it too);
  • when the account was created and when it was last used;
  • which emails you switched on or off, when, in which language, region and app version, from which screen, and which exact wording you were shown. These are kept so that Nous can prove you asked, as the law requires. Every version of that wording, in every language, is kept.

There are two kinds of email, each with its own switch, and both start off: the Monthly AI News Brief (once a month, the stories that mattered most) and Announcements about Nous (new features and news about the app, a few times a year). Creating the account does not switch either on. Nothing has been sent yet. Before any newsletter or announcement, Nous will email you first, once, so that you know it is coming and can stop it.

The account is used for nothing else. It is not linked to what you read, what you follow, your notifications or your subscription, it is never given to RevenueCat, and it is never used for advertising by anyone else, sold or shared with data brokers. You can see everything it keeps in the app, under Account.

Signing out, under Account, signs out only the device you are holding: it unlinks that copy of the app and keeps the account. If you stop using Sign in with Apple with Nous in your Apple ID settings, or delete your Apple ID, the app signs out on your device and Apple tells Nous, and the account is then deleted.

The account is kept until you delete it. To delete it, open the app's settings, then Account, then Delete account. Everything listed above is deleted at once and Apple is told to disconnect Nous from your Apple ID (if Apple cannot be reached, Nous keeps trying for up to 30 days, with the token encrypted and linked to nothing); backups of the server's database keep it for at most 72 hours more. One thing is kept on purpose: the record of each email you switched on and off, with a final “switched off” for any still on, unlinked from the account. It holds no address, no account and no identifier, only that someone agreed to an email under a given wording on one date and stopped it on another, and it is kept for three years as proof of consent, then deleted. Deleting the app's data on the server (below) deletes the account too, if that copy of the app is signed in to one.

Only if you turn on notifications

The daily notification is off until you switch it on. When you do, the app registers with the server and it stores: the notification token Apple issues for that install, whether it is a development or production token, your device's language and region setting, your device's time zone, the time of day you chose, whether the notification is on or off, the app's version number, the key identifier above, when the registration was made and last refreshed, which edition was last delivered and when, a count of consecutive delivery failures, and, if the app proved a Nous+ subscription when it registered, the date that subscription ends. From version 2.0 it also stores whether the app wants a silent update shortly before your notification, and which edition that update last named and when. That silent update carries only the edition's date: it lets the app download the day's edition, and for Nous+ prepare the spoken brief, before you open it. Nothing is shown and nothing is sent back about it.

Switching notifications off deletes that row immediately: it is a delete, not a flag. If you never switch them off, the row is deleted 180 days after the app last contacted the server.

Only if you turn on breaking alerts (Nous+, version 2.0 and later)

Breaking alerts are off until you switch them on. When you do, the app sends the server the companies, models and topics you follow, so that it can tell you when something major happens to one of them. They are sent as entries from Nous's own list of companies, models and topics, which is the same for every reader. The server stores them next to the notification record above, keyed by that install's notification token, together with how many alerts a day you chose (one or two), your quiet hours, the date your Nous+ subscription ends (the only thing read from the subscription receipt, which is then discarded, as described below), and how many alerts it has sent you today. To avoid sending you the same story twice, it also keeps a short record of which stories it alerted that token about.

The list of what you follow is not sent at all while breaking alerts are off. Switching breaking alerts off deletes it immediately. It is also deleted with the notification record (when you switch notifications off, when you use Delete all my data, or 180 days after the app last contacted the server), and 30 days after your Nous+ subscription ends if you have not renewed it. The record of stories already sent is deleted after 30 days.

Only if the voice is downloaded through Cloudflare (version 1.2 and later)

When the app fetches the voice for Listen from Cloudflare (see Listening), it first asks the server for download links. So that one copy of the app cannot be used to download the voice over and over, the server keeps, per install and per day: the key identifier above, the date, how many download links it issued that day, and the total size of the files those links cover. These rows are deleted after 30 days.

If a copy of the app is used to request far more downloads than the app ever makes, the server may also record its key identifier, the reason and the date, and refuse it further links. That record is deleted with your data, or 180 days after the app last contacted the server.

Subscriptions and payment

Nous+ is sold through Apple's In-App Purchase system as two plans: 1 week free, then either US$2.99 per month or US$24.99 per year, until cancelled. Apple takes the payment. Your name, your Apple ID and your payment details go to Apple and are never sent to this server, which has no way to ask for them.

To decide whether a request is entitled, the app sends the signed receipt Apple gives it. The server checks Apple's signature on that receipt and reads six things from it: the app it was issued for, which subscription was bought, when it expires, whether it was refunded, whether it came from Apple's test environment, and the reference number described next. The receipt itself is then discarded. It is not written to this server's database, and no transaction identifier, purchase history, price or Apple ID is stored there. (The separate purchase record kept by RevenueCat is described under Purchase records below.) The one exception, from version 2.0, is the keyed fingerprint described under Give a free week, and only if you use it.

The subscription reference number

So that one subscription unlocks one copy of the app rather than being passed around, the app generates a random reference number when you subscribe and hands it to Apple as part of the purchase. It is not derived from anything: not from your Apple ID, not from your device, not from you. Apple echoes it back inside every signed receipt for that subscription, and the server stores it next to the key identifier of the copy of the app that first presented it, along with when that happened, when it was last seen, and how many times it has moved to a different copy.

That is the whole of it: a random number, an anonymous key identifier and three dates. It is deleted when you delete your data, and automatically after a year in which the subscription is never presented.

The one other thing kept is that end date, and only if you have notifications or breaking alerts on: it is stored on the notification and breaking-alert records described above, and nowhere else.

Give a free week (version 2.0 and later)

A Nous+ subscriber can send a friend a free week. The app asks the server for a code, and the server hands out one of a stock of one-time offer codes issued by Apple, as a link you can share. The usable code is erased from the server as it is handed out; only a one-way fingerprint of it remains, so the same code is never handed out twice.

So that each subscription can give at most three free weeks in 30 days, the server stores, next to each code it hands out, the time and a keyed fingerprint of your subscription's original transaction number. The fingerprint is made with a secret key held only by the server and cannot be turned back into the transaction number; the number itself is never stored. It is not linked to your copy of the app, your key identifier or your notification record. These rows are deleted 400 days after the code was handed out.

The server never learns who receives the code. Your friend redeems it with Apple, which handles that redemption as it handles any purchase. Redeeming any offer code in the app (Settings, Redeem code) opens Apple's own redemption screen; nothing about it passes through this server.

Purchase records: RevenueCat (version 2.0 and later)

From version 2.0, the app uses RevenueCat, a subscription service, to keep a record of Nous+ purchases, so that we can see how many people start a trial, subscribe, renew or cancel. RevenueCat does not take payments, does not decide what you can read, and never sees your name, email address, Apple ID or payment details: Apple handles the payment, and this server still decides what is unlocked, exactly as described above.

Each time the app opens, whether or not you subscribe, the RevenueCat code inside the app contacts RevenueCat with the details listed at the end of this paragraph, and RevenueCat answers with what it holds for your copy of the app (nothing, if you never bought). When you buy, restore or redeem Nous+, or when a purchase made elsewhere reaches the app (a renewal, a redeemed offer code), it also sends RevenueCat the signed purchase record Apple gave it. That record holds which plan was bought, the price and currency, the purchase and renewal dates, Apple's transaction numbers for it, whether it is a trial or an offer, and the App Store country. Every one of these contacts carries a random identifier that RevenueCat creates for your copy of the app, the identifier Apple gives this app for your device (the “identifier for vendor”, which is the same for every app from the same developer and changes when they are all deleted), the app version, the iOS version, the device model and your language settings. Your IP address reaches RevenueCat because that is how the request is delivered. Apple also tells RevenueCat directly when a subscription renews, lapses or is refunded.

The app never gives RevenueCat a name, an email address or the optional account, never attaches anything else to the record, and never collects an advertising identifier. RevenueCat is our processor: it keeps the record for us, in the United States, under its data processing terms and with the safeguards the GDPR requires for transfers outside the EU. It is used only for subscription statistics and to send us a short notice of a new subscription, never for advertising or tracking, never to build a profile, and never combined with data from other apps. It is kept for as long as Nous uses RevenueCat, unless you ask for it to be deleted (see Deleting your data). It is separate from this server: Delete all my data in the app does not reach it.

You can switch it off. Settings, Data and offline, Send crash reports covers this too. While it is off, the app sends no purchase record to RevenueCat, and from the next time the app is opened it does not contact RevenueCat at all. Switching it off does not change your subscription or what you can read.

You can view or cancel the subscription at any time at https://apps.apple.com/account/subscriptions. See the subscription terms for the rest.

IP addresses

Your IP address reaches the server, because that is how the internet delivers a request. It is used for one thing: counting requests per minute so that the free, unauthenticated part of the handshake cannot be abused. The count lives in memory, in one-minute windows, and IPv6 addresses are truncated to their network prefix before being counted. No IP address is written to the database, ever.

That covers the app's requests to the server. A crash report is a request to a different service, described under crash reports below, and that service is set not to store the address it arrives from. Asking for this web page is a separate thing again, handled by a separate host: see the website you are reading this on.

Diagnostic logs

The server writes a line per authenticated request containing whether the signature verified, the reason if it did not, the use counter, the path that was requested, and the first eight characters of the key identifier (a prefix, so a log line cannot be tied back to a row on its own). Turning notifications on or off writes a line with the language, time zone and time of day chosen. From version 2.0, two kinds of path are shortened before they are written: a request about breaking alerts is logged without the notification token in it, and opening a Storyline is logged without the company, model or topic it was about.

Nothing written by this server records a search term: the line records which endpoint was called, never the query. A search does travel to the server inside the web address of the request, so it can appear in the connection records the hosting provider keeps for its own operations, in the same way any website's search does. It is not stored here, not kept in the database, and not associated with an install.

These lines go to the hosting provider's log stream and are kept for as long as that provider keeps them. They are read to diagnose faults and are not analysed for anything else.

Crash reports

If the app crashes, it sends a crash report the next time it is opened, so that the fault can be found and fixed. Reports go to Sentry, a crash reporting service, and are stored in the EU, in Frankfurt. Apart from the purchase records described above, this is the one thing the app sends anywhere other than the server described above.

A report is technical. It contains where in the app's code the failure happened, the app's version and build number, the iOS version, and facts about the device such as its model, processor type, memory, screen size, language and region setting, and time zone. The same kind of report is sent in the rare case that the app has to rebuild its local storage at launch, with the database error code that caused it, and when a screen shows an error that suggests the service itself is broken, with a short fixed code naming the kind of failure.

From version 1.2.3, the app also sends performance measurements to Sentry: how long each screen takes to appear and to show its stories, how smoothly it draws, and when the app stops responding or is closed by the system for using too much memory. With a report, it sends a short trail of the screens opened before it and, for each story opened from a list, which list it was in, its topic (such as Research or Open Source, the same for every reader) and its position in that list. That is all a trail contains: never a story's title or address, and nothing you typed.

A report does not contain the title, text or address of anything you read or saved, anything you searched for or typed, any web address the app requested, a screenshot, or the key identifier and reference number described earlier. The reporting software would normally add two identifiers of its own that stay the same for one copy of the app; both are removed on your device before a report is sent, so two reports cannot be tied to each other, to a row on the server, or to you. Every other optional kind of collection the software offers is switched off: no usage or session counts, no recording of the screen, no record of the web requests the app makes, and no identifier sent to any other service.

You can switch it off. Settings, Data and offline, Send crash reports. It is on unless you turn it off; while it is off, nothing described in this section is sent at all, and turning it off stops reporting at once.

Sentry receives your IP address when a report arrives, because that is how the internet delivers a request, and is set not to store it. Reports are deleted automatically after no more than 90 days. Because a report carries nothing that identifies you, there is no way to find or delete one person's reports on request, which is a consequence of collecting nothing rather than an obstacle put in the way.

Kept on your device only (version 2.0 and later)

  • What you follow. The companies, models and topics you follow are stored on your device. They mark stories in Today and shape your spoken brief there. They leave the device only if you turn on breaking alerts.
  • Your reading history and weekly recap. To show your week in Nous, the app keeps a record on your device of which stories you opened and listened to and which editions and briefs you finished, for eight weeks. The recap is worked out on the device. Neither is ever sent anywhere, and Settings, Clear reading history deletes the record at once.
  • The spoken brief prepared overnight. For Nous+, the app may prepare the next morning's spoken brief at night while your device is charging, so that it starts at once. It is written, translated and read on your device, exactly as described under Listening, and the audio stays on your device.
  • Siri, Shortcuts and the Action button. The app tells iOS which actions it offers, such as playing the brief or opening today's edition. When you use one, iOS runs it in the app on your device, and the app sends nothing extra to this server because of it. What you say to Siri is handled by Apple under Apple's own privacy policy; Nous never receives your voice or what you said.

What is never collected

No name, phone number, postal address, contacts, photos, calendar, microphone, camera, health or fitness data, or precise or approximate location. No email address, unless you create the optional account. No advertising identifier. No fingerprinting. The app contains no advertising network and no third-party tracker of any kind, and nothing in it records what you read or tap. No data leaves the app except the requests to this server, the crash reports described above, from version 1.2 the download of the voice described under Listening, and from version 2.0 the contact with RevenueCat described under purchase records, each time the app opens, unless you switch it off, and Sign in with Apple, only if you create the optional account.

Nothing is sold. Nothing is shared with data brokers. Nothing is used to build a profile or to target advertising, here or anywhere else.

The website you are reading this on

This policy, the subscription terms and the support page are published as static files on GitHub Pages, separately from the app and its server, so that they keep answering even when the server does not. Asking for one of these pages is therefore a request to GitHub, and GitHub records that request, including your IP address, in its own logs, under its own privacy practices, in the same way that any web host records a request for any page.

That is the whole of GitHub's involvement. It holds no app data: none of the tables above, not a notification record, not a subscription reference number. These pages carry no analytics, no cookies, no scripts, and nothing loaded from any other site, so reading them tells the app nothing about you and tells the author of this policy nothing about you either.

The news itself, and the summaries

Nous reads publicly available sources: RSS and Atom feeds published by news outlets, research labs and companies; Hacker News; arXiv; and public posts from a curated list of accounts on X. It ranks them and writes short summaries using a large language model, reached through OpenRouter.

All of that happens on a schedule, on the server, before any device asks for anything. Your reading is not part of it. Nothing you tap, search or open is sent to OpenRouter or to any other model provider, and no request from your device causes a server-side model to run. What is sent to OpenRouter is the text of published articles, which was already public.

Because the sources are third parties, Nous does not control what they publish. Summaries are generated automatically and can be wrong. If something in an edition is inaccurate, offensive or should not be there, write to hi@alp.me and it will be removed.

Listening (version 1.2 and later)

Listen reads a spoken summary of the day, one, two or five minutes long. All of it happens on your device.

  • The summary is written on your device. Where your device supports it, Apple Intelligence writes it with Apple's on-device model; this app never uses Apple's cloud models. Elsewhere it is put together from the edition's own summaries. Either way, it is made only from the day's published edition.
  • If you listen in another language, it is translated on your device by Apple's translation feature. iOS may download that language to your device the first time.
  • It is read aloud by the Nous voice, a speech model that runs on your device. The voice is synthetic: it is generated by a machine and is not a recording of a person.

Nothing you listen to, and none of your listening choices (the length, the voice, the speed, the language), is sent anywhere. They are stored on your device and nowhere else.

Downloading the voice

The voice is too large to include in the app, so it is downloaded once, about 100 MB, the first time it is needed, and updated occasionally. It comes from one of two places:

  • Apple, which delivers it the way it delivers the app itself.
  • Cloudflare R2, in the EU. The app first sends the server one signed request, like any other, and the server answers with links that stop working after 24 hours. Your device then downloads the files from Cloudflare, which receives your IP address because that is how the internet delivers a file. Access logging is not switched on for these files, so no record of the download comes back to Nous.

The files are the same for every copy of the app and contain nothing about you. Before downloading, the app checks that your device has enough free space; that figure never leaves your device. You can remove the voice at any time in the app's settings.

Deleting your data

You need no account, but there are rows, and all of them can go, from inside the app, without asking anyone:

  • Delete account, under Account in the app's settings, deletes the optional account and everything kept with it, immediately, and tells Apple to disconnect Nous from your Apple ID. Switching an email off stops that email at once and keeps the account.
  • Delete all my data, in the app's settings, deletes everything the server holds about your copy of the app in one action, and the optional account first, if this copy of the app is signed in to one: the key identifier, its public key and counter, any notification record, any breaking-alert record and the list of what you follow that came with it, the subscription reference number, and any record of voice downloads. It happens immediately, and the app tells you what was deleted. The app then starts fresh with a new, unrelated identifier the next time it is opened. Your subscription is not affected: it belongs to your Apple ID, not to this app, and it will simply attach itself to the new copy. One thing outlives the deletion briefly: a one-way fingerprint of the subscription reference number, with how many times that subscription has moved between copies of the app and when it last moved, and nothing that identifies your copy of the app. It stops a deletion being used to pass one subscription between several people, and it is deleted when the subscription attaches to your new copy, or after about two days at the latest.
  • Switching notifications off deletes the notification record on its own, immediately, with any breaking-alert record, and leaves the rest.
  • Switching breaking alerts off deletes the list of what you follow from the server immediately.
  • Give a free week records are not tied to your copy of the app, so they cannot be found from it; they are deleted automatically 400 days after each code was handed out.
  • Deleting the app without using any of these controls leaves the key identifier, which is deleted automatically 180 days later, and the optional account, if you created one, which stays until you delete it (you can do that from a new copy of the app by signing in again).
  • Purchase records at RevenueCat (version 2.0 and later) are not held by this server, so Delete all my data does not reach them. Write to hi@alp.me with the order number from Apple's purchase receipt email, and the record will be found by that number and deleted.

If you would rather ask a person, write to hi@alp.me. Because nothing held here identifies you, a mailed request has to name the identifier the app shows in its settings. There is no other way to tell which row is yours, which is a consequence of collecting nothing rather than an obstacle put in the way. If you created the optional account, a request about it can simply name its email address.

Your rights

If you are in the EU, the UK, or a US state with a privacy statute, you have rights to access, correct, delete and export what is held about you, and to object to its processing. Exercise any of them by writing to hi@alp.me. The practical answer is usually short, because the honest answer to “what do you hold about me” is the rows described above and nothing else. Crash reports are not part of that answer because they cannot be tied to a person or to a copy of the app.

For the optional account you can also see everything it keeps, in the app, under Account. The legal basis for the account itself is that you asked for it (performing what you asked for, GDPR Article 6(1)(b)); the legal basis for each email is your consent (Article 6(1)(a)), which you can withdraw at any time with its switch, without affecting what happened before.

The legal basis for holding the rest, under the GDPR, is legitimate interest: running the service, preventing abuse, and delivering a notification you asked for. Crash reports and performance measurements rest on the same basis: finding and fixing faults in the app, and you can switch them off in the app. The contact with RevenueCat and the purchase records it keeps rest on it too, and the same switch turns them off: knowing how the subscription that pays for the service is doing. They hold no name, email address or Apple ID, and you can ask for them to be deleted, as described above. There is no automated decision-making about you.

The things switched on by an explicit choice are the daily notification and, from version 2.0, breaking alerts and the two email switches of the optional account. Withdrawing any of them is its switch in the app: switching a notification off stops it and deletes its record, and switching an email off stops it at once, in one action. Nothing else requires or relies on your consent, so there is nothing else to withdraw, and the rest can still be deleted, as described above.
